# 4
(a) Find the area of an ideal hyperbolic triangle, with a bit of justification. (Hint: try not to do any integrals.)

**Proof**

For a geodesic triangle $T$ in the hyperbolic plane (with constant curvature $K=-1$), the Gauss–Bonnet theorem states

$$\int_T K\ dA + \sum_{i=1}^3 (\pi-\alpha_i) = 2\pi\chi(T),$$

where $\alpha_i$ are the interior angles. Since $K=-1$ and the Euler characteristic $\chi(T)$ of a disk is $1$, and because geodesics have geodesic curvature $\kappa_g=0$, this simplifies to

$$-\operatorname{Area}(T) + 3\pi - (\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3) = 2\pi.$$

Rearranging gives the angle-defect formula:

$$\operatorname{Area}(T) = \pi - (\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3).$$

An ideal triangle has all three vertices on the boundary at infinity. The interior angle at a boundary point is $0$, so $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha_3=0$. Hence

$$\operatorname{Area} = \pi.$$

(b) Argue that any triangle in hyperbolic space has area bounded above by that of an ideal one.

**Proof**

From the angle-defect formula above, the area of any triangle is $\pi - (\alpha+\beta+\gamma)$. Because interior angles satisfy $\alpha,\beta,\gamma \ge 0$, we immediately have
$$\operatorname{Area}(\Delta) \le \pi,$$
with equality if and only if $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=0$, i.e., precisely for an ideal triangle.

# 6
Suppose that $p=\gamma(0)$ and $q=\gamma(b)$ are not conjugate along a geodesic $\gamma$. Show that the 2-point boundary problem for the Jacobi equation

$D_t^2 J+R(J, \dot{\gamma}) \dot{\gamma}=0, \quad J(0)=v, \quad J(b)=w$

is uniquely solvable for any $v \in T_p M$ and $w \in T_q M$.

**Proof**

The Jacobi equation along $\gamma$ is a second-order linear ordinary differential equation for a vector field $J$ along $\gamma$. In a parallel frame along $\gamma$, it takes the form $\ddot{J} + R(t)J = 0$, where $R(t)$ is a symmetric matrix depending on the curvature tensor. The space $\mathcal{J}$ of solutions (Jacobi fields) is a real vector space of dimension $2n$, where $n = \dim M$.

Define the linear map  

$\Phi : \mathcal{J} \to T_p M \times T_q M, \quad \Phi(J) = (J(0), J(b)).$

Both the domain and codomain have dimension $2n$. The kernel of $\Phi$ consists of Jacobi fields that vanish at both endpoints:  

$`\ker \Phi = \{ J \in \mathcal{J} : J(0) = 0,\ J(b) = 0 \}.`$

By definition, $p$ and $q$ are not conjugate along $\gamma$ if and only if there is no nonzero Jacobi field vanishing at both ends, i.e., $`\ker \Phi = \{0\}`$. Thus, under the given non-conjugacy assumption, $\Phi$ is injective. Since $\Phi$ is a linear map between finite-dimensional vector spaces of the same dimension, injectivity implies surjectivity. Therefore, for any $(v, w) \in T_p M \times T_q M$, there exists a unique Jacobi field $J \in \mathcal{J}$ such that $J(0) = v$ and $J(b) = w$.

This establishes unique solvability of the two-point boundary problem.
