# 3.42
Let $R$ be a commutative ring, $X$ a variable, $F:=a_0+a_1 X+\cdots+a_n X^n$.
1. Prove $F$ is nilpotent if and only if $a_0, \ldots, a_n$ are nilpotent.
    
    **Proof**

    The nilpotent elements in a commutative ring form an ideal, by the binomial theorem, and nilpotent elements of $R$ are nilpotent in $R[X]$. Therefore if a polynomial in $R[X]$ has coefficients that are nilpotent in $R$, then the polynomial is nilpotent in $R[X]$.

    Conversely, suppose $f \in R[X]$ is nilpotent. To show all the coefficients of $f$ are nilpotent in $R$, we may focus on nonzero $f$ and induct on $\deg f$. The case $\deg f = 0$ is easy. Suppose $n \geq 1$ and the result is true for all polynomials of degree less than $n$. For a nilpotent polynomial $f = a_0 + a_1 X + \cdots + a_n X^n$ of degree $n$, say

   $f^k = 0$

   for some $k \geq 1$. Looking at the coefficient of $X^{n k}$ on both sides tells us $a_n^k = 0$. Therefore $a_n$ is nilpotent in $R$. Since the nilpotent elements in $R[X]$ form an ideal,

   $f - a_n X^n = a_0 + a_1 X + \cdots + a_{n-1} X^{n-1}$

   is nilpotent in $R[X]$ and is either 0 or has degree less than $n$. Either way, by induction all $a_i$ for $i \leq n-1$ are nilpotent, so all the coefficients of $f$ are nilpotent.

2. Prove $F$ is a unit if and only if $a_0$ is a unit and $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ are nilpotent.
    
    **Proof**

   ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $f$ is a unit in $R[X]$. Then there exists $g \in R[X]$ such that $fg = 1$. Comparing constant terms yields $a_0$ times the constant term of $g$ equals 1, so $a_0 \in R^\times$.

   To show $a_i$ is nilpotent for $i \geq 1$, let $\mathfrak{p}$ be any prime ideal of $R$. Reducing coefficients modulo $\mathfrak{p}$ gives $\overline{f} \overline{g} = 1$ in $(R/\mathfrak{p})[X]$. Since $R/\mathfrak{p}$ is an integral domain, the degree of a product of non-zero polynomials is the sum of their degrees. From $\overline{f} \overline{g} = 1$, we get $\deg(\overline{f}) + \deg(\overline{g}) = \deg(1) = 0$. Since degrees are non-negative, this implies $\deg(\overline{f}) = 0$. Thus, $\overline{f}$ is a non-zero constant polynomial, whose constant term is a unit in $R/\mathfrak{p}$. Therefore, $a_i \in \mathfrak{p}$ for all $i \geq 1$. As this holds for every prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, each $a_i$ ($i \geq 1$) belongs to the nilradical of $R$ and is therefore nilpotent.
    
    ($\Leftarrow$) Conversely, suppose $a_0 \in R^\times$ and $a_i$ nilpotent for $i \geq 1$. Write $f = a_0 + g$ where $g = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i X^i$. By (1) a polynomial is nilpotent if and only if all its coefficients are nilpotent, $g$ is nilpotent in $R[X]$. Let $k$ be such that $g^k = 0$. Then $f = a_0 (1 + a_0^{-1} g)$. Set $u = a_0^{-1} g$, which is nilpotent ($u^k = a_0^{-k} g^k = 0$). The inverse of $1 + u$ is the finite geometric sum $\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} (-1)^j u^j$, since $(1 + u) \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} (-1)^j u^j = 1 - (-1)^k u^k = 1$. Thus, $1 + u$ is a unit, and $f$ is a unit as the product of units $a_0$ and $1 + u$.

# Problem A
An ideal $I$ is said to be nilpotent if $I^n=0$ for some $n$. Show that if $I \subset R$ is nilpotent, then $I \subset \mathrm{nil}(R)$.

Conversely, show that if $I \subset \mathrm{nil}(R)$ is a finitely generated ideal (that is, $I=\left(a_1, \ldots, a_k\right)$ for some $k$ and some $a_i$), then $I$ is nilpotent.

**Proof**

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $I$ is a nilpotent ideal, so $I^n=0$ for some integer $n \ge 1$. Let $a \in I$. Then $a^n \in I^n = \{0\}$, which means $a^n=0$. Thus, $a$ is a nilpotent element. The nilradical, $\mathrm{nil}(R)$, is the set of all nilpotent elements of $R$, so $a \in \mathrm{nil}(R)$. Since $a$ was an arbitrary element of $I$, we have $I \subseteq \mathrm{nil}(R)$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $I = (a_1, \ldots, a_k)$ is a finitely generated ideal and $I \subseteq \mathrm{nil}(R)$. Since each generator $a_i \in I \subseteq \mathrm{nil}(R)$, each $a_i$ is nilpotent. This means for each $i \in \{1, \ldots, k\}$, there exists an integer $n_i \ge 1$ such that $a_i^{n_i} = 0$.
Let $N = \sum_{i=1}^k (n_i - 1) + 1$. An arbitrary element of $I^N$ is a sum of terms of the form $r \cdot m$ where $r \in R$ and $m$ is a monomial $a_1^{e_1} \cdots a_k^{e_k}$ with $\sum_{i=1}^k e_i = N$.
For any such monomial, there must be at least one index $j$ such that $e_j \ge n_j$. If not, then $e_i \le n_i - 1$ for all $i$, which would imply $\sum e_i \le \sum (n_i-1) = N-1$, a contradiction.
Since $e_j \ge n_j$, we have $a_j^{e_j} = a_j^{n_j} a_j^{e_j-n_j} = 0 \cdot a_j^{e_j-n_j} = 0$. Thus, the monomial $a_1^{e_1} \cdots a_k^{e_k}$ is zero.
As all generators of $I^N$ (which are these monomials) are zero, we have $I^N = \{0\}$. Therefore, $I$ is a nilpotent ideal.
