# 1.
Give an example of a complete, noncompact Riemannian manifold where every point lies on a closed geodesic.

**Proof.**

A flat two-dimensional cylinder $S^1 \times \mathbb{R}$ with the product metric $d\theta^2+dh^2$, is a complete, noncompact Riemannian manifold. For any point $p=(x,y) \in S^1 \times \mathbb{R}$, the circle $`S^1 \times \{y\}`$ is a closed geodesic containing $p$.

# 2.
Prove that any locally minimizing admissible path is a critical point of $L(\cdot)$.

**Proof**

Let $\gamma$ be a locally minimizing admissible path. By definition, for any admissible variation $\gamma_s$ of $\gamma$ (with $\gamma_0 = \gamma$), there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $s$ with $|s| < \delta$, we have
$$
L(\gamma_s) \ge L(\gamma).
$$
This means the function $f(s) = L(\gamma_s)$ has a local minimum at $s=0$. Assuming $f(s)$ is a smooth function, a necessary condition for a local minimum at an interior point of the domain is that the derivative at that point is zero. Thus,
$$
\left.\frac{d}{ds}L(\gamma_s)\right|_{s=0}=0.
$$
By definition, this means that $\gamma$ is a critical point of the length functional $L(\cdot)$.

# 3.
Prove that if $(M, g)$ is connected and there exists $p \in M$ such that $\exp_p$ is defined on all of $T_p M$, then $M$ is complete.

**Proof**

We aim to show that $(M,g)$ is a complete metric space, this is equivalent to showing that any closed and bounded set in $M$ is compact. The proof of this relies on the following lemma.

**Lemma:** For any $q \in M$, there exists a geodesic of length $d(p, q)$ connecting $p$ and $q$.

Let $K \subset M$ be a bounded closed set. Then there exists a constant $C>0$ so that $d(p, k)<C$ for all $k \in K$. According to the lemma, $K \subset \exp_p\left(\overline{B_C(0)}\right)$. The set $\overline{B_C(0)}$ is a closed ball in $T_p M$, hence compact. Since $\exp_p$ is continuous, the image $\exp_p\left(\overline{B_C(0)}\right)$ is also compact. Thus $K$, as a closed subset of a compact set, is compact.

Proof of lemma:

Denote $r=d(p, q)$. There exists $0<\delta<r$ so that the exponential map $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism from $B_\delta(0) \in T_p M$ to $B(p, \delta) \in M$.

Note that the geodesic sphere $S(p, \delta)=\exp_p\left(S_\delta(0)\right)$ is compact. Since the distance function is continuous, there exists $p_0 \in S(p, \delta)$ so that

$$
d\left(p_0, q\right)=\inf _{p' \in S(p, \delta)} d\left(p', q\right)
$$

Let $\gamma$ be the normal geodesic from $p$ to $p_0$. By hypothesis, since $\exp_p$ is defined on all of $T_p M$, $\gamma$ is defined over $\mathbb{R}$. Let

$$
A=\{s \in[\delta, r] \mid d(\gamma(s), q)=r-s\} .
$$

We will show $\sup A=r$, which implies $\gamma(r)=q$.
To prove this, we first notice that $\delta \in A$. This follows from the fact that $p_0=\gamma(\delta)$ is the closest point to $q$ on the sphere $S(p,\delta)$, which implies:

$$
r=d(p, q) = \delta + d(\gamma(\delta), q)
$$

From this, $d(\gamma(\delta), q) = r - \delta$, so $\delta \in A$.

So $A$ is nonempty.

Secondly, it's easy to see that $A$ is closed, since the function

$$
f(s)=d(\gamma(s), q)-r+s
$$

is continuous and $A=f^{-1}(0) \cap[\delta, r]$.

Now let $s_0=\sup A$. Since $A$ is nonempty and closed, $s_0 \in A$. Suppose $s_0<r$. Then by repeating the previous argument, we know that there exists $0<\delta'<r-s_0$ and $p_0' \in S\left(\gamma\left(s_0\right), \delta'\right)$ so that

$$
d\left(p_0', q\right)=\min _{p' \in S\left(\gamma\left(s_0\right), \delta'\right)} d\left(p', q\right)=d\left(\gamma\left(s_0\right), q\right)-\delta' .
$$

Since $s_0 \in A$, we get

$$
d\left(p_0', q\right)=r-s_0-\delta' .
$$

So by the triangle inequality,

$$
d\left(p_0', p\right) \geq d(p, q)-d\left(p_0', q\right)=r-\left(r-s_0-\delta'\right)=s_0+\delta' .
$$

On the other hand, the curve $\tilde{\gamma}$ by connecting $p$ to $\gamma\left(s_0\right)$ along $\gamma$ and then connecting $\gamma\left(s_0\right)$ to $p_0'$ by the "radial" minimal geodesic has length exactly $s_0+\delta'$. So $\tilde{\gamma}$, with the arc-length parametrization, must be a geodesic. Obviously $\tilde{\gamma}$ has to coincide with $\gamma$. In other words, $p_0'=\gamma\left(s_0+\delta'\right)$. As a consequence,

$$
d\left(\gamma\left(s_0+\delta'\right), q\right)=r-\left(s_0+\delta'\right),
$$

i.e. $s_0+\delta' \in A$. This conflicts with the fact that $s_0=\sup A$.
